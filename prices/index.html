Search above, pick a kind of doctor to see their price menu, or choose from the list. You'll get a best guess for the total price, the range most people fall in, and what each part of the bill is.

How to read an estimate

It's the total price, not your share

The estimate is what the doctor and the facility are paid in total. With insurance, what you owe depends on your deductible and coinsurance: if you haven't met your deductible, expect to owe most of it. Paying yourself, a fair cash quote lands inside the range.

Where it's done matters most

The same procedure costs least in a doctor's office, more in a surgery center, and most in a hospital outpatient department, because each adds its own facility fee. You can ask where it will be done.

It is an estimate

Medicare's figures are real payments. The step up to private prices uses national research averages, some of them several years old. The figure for doctors is applied to every kind of clinician here, including therapists, physical therapists and chiropractors, and real prices vary by city, insurer and clinician. Use this to know whether a quote is reasonable, not as the quote.

How we come up with these numbers

  1. We start with Medicare's price. Medicare publishes what it paid for every procedure: the doctor's fee, the surgery center's fee and the hospital's fee. These are real payments from 2024 (surgery center and scan prices are October 2026), counting both Medicare's share and the patient's.123
  2. We scale it up to private prices. Private insurers pay more than Medicare, and studies have measured how much more: about 143% of Medicare's price for doctors,4 170% for surgery centers and 279% for hospital outpatient departments.5 We multiply each part of the bill by its figure. That is the best guess.
  3. The range uses the lowest and highest figures. For doctors (118% to 179%) and hospitals (150% to 358%), those are the lowest and highest averages the studies found.4
  4. One range is our own assumption. No study gives a national range for surgery centers, so we set one ourselves: from Medicare's own price (100%) up to what hospitals average (279%). Some states' surgery centers average no more than Medicare's price, and on average surgery centers are paid well below hospitals for the same services.5 Every estimate that includes a surgery center marks that range with a star.
  5. We add the parts up and round. Every estimate shows this arithmetic under “How we got this number”, so you can check it.

Getting a real number

  • Give them the billing code. Each estimate here shows one. Asking “what is your price for code 15823?” gets further than asking what a procedure costs.
  • Ask for the self-pay or cash price, and whether it covers the facility and anesthesia or only the doctor.
  • Ask for a Good Faith Estimate. If you are uninsured or not using insurance, federal law (the No Surprises Act) requires providers to give you a written estimate when you schedule care or ask for one.7
  • Ask whether it can be done in the office or a surgery center instead of a hospital.

Disclaimer

Everything on this page is for information only. It is not medical, financial or legal advice. What Works? does not guarantee that these estimates are accurate, complete, or right for your situation: they are best guesses worked out from public data, not prices from any doctor, hospital or insurer.

Always confirm a price directly with the doctor, hospital or facility before you schedule. To find out what you will owe with insurance, contact your plan and ask about your deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, copay and coinsurance.
