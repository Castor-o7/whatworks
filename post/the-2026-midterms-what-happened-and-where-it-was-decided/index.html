The 2026 midterms: what happened, and where it was decided

Democrats took the House and Senate in a wave, but most seats were settled long before November. Here is the map before and after, the races decided in primaries, and the ones still being counted.

By Josh · November 4, 2026

Democrats took both chambers of Congress on November 3, 2026, and it was not close. CBS News, NBC News and CNN all projected Democratic control of the House and the Senate before dawn on November 4.123 Counting is still under way, so every number below is a Wednesday-morning snapshot, not a certified result.

Chamber Before Nov 3 After Nov 3 (projected, Nov 4 morning) Still uncalled
Senate (100) 53 R, 47 D (45 D + 2 I) 54 D, 44 R 2 (Alaska, Mississippi)
House (435) 219 R, 214 D, 2 vacant D at least 258 (NBC); CBS estimates 259, range 251 to 268 about 53 to 75
Governors (36 up) GOP held a majority nationally Democrats flipped 5 (TX, GA, NV, OH, IA) 2 (Alaska, Kansas)

The Senate flipped on seven Republican-held seats and zero Democratic losses: Iowa, Kansas, Maine, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas.4 In the House, no sitting Democrat lost, and mid-decade Republican redistricting in Texas, Florida, Missouri, North Carolina and Ohio did not stop the wave.5

Voters treated the election as a referendum on President Trump and on prices. CBS exit polls put his approval at 38 percent among midterm voters, with about nine in ten calling the cost of living a major or crucial factor, and majorities saying his tariffs hurt the economy and that military action against Iran was the wrong decision.65 Vice President Vance’s own verdict, quoted by CBS: “The reason we lost is the war.”7

The leadership fallout started within hours. Speaker Mike Johnson told his staff he will not seek the minority leader job, and Jim Jordan wants it. Hakeem Jeffries is poised to become the first Black Speaker of the House, and John Thune is expected to run for Senate minority leader.2

The Senate: 53 to 47 Republican becomes 54 to 44 Democratic

Thirty-five seats were on the ballot: the 33 regular seats plus special elections in Ohio (JD Vance’s old seat) and Florida (Marco Rubio’s). Republicans were defending 22 of them and Democrats 13.8 Democrats needed a net gain of four to take the chamber. They got seven, and gave back none.

The seven flips, in order of the call (vote shares from Fox News at 11:50 AM ET; call times from CBS):97

  • North Carolina (8:30 PM): Roy Cooper (D) 58.7, Michael Whatley (R) 39.1. The former governor becomes the state’s first Democratic senator in almost two decades. Open seat; Thom Tillis retired.
  • South Carolina (10:03 PM): Annie Andrews (D) 51.1, Darline Graham (R) 46.7. Lindsey Graham died on July 11. Gov. McMaster appointed his sister Darline at Trump’s recommendation, and she won a special GOP primary before losing to Andrews, a pediatrician and the first woman South Carolina has elected to the Senate.23 Cook had rated the seat Solid Republican in June.
  • Texas (10:59 PM): James Talarico (D) 55.1, Ken Paxton (R) 43.0. Trump endorsed Paxton over Sen. John Cornyn in the primary. Talarico’s win is the first statewide GOP loss in Texas in more than 30 years.2
  • Ohio special (11:15 PM): Sherrod Brown (D) 50.0, Jon Husted (R) 45.2. Brown returns two years after losing Ohio’s other seat.
  • Kansas (11:29 PM): Adam Hamilton (D) 50.9, Roger Marshall (R) 46.1. Kansas elected a Democrat to the Senate for the first time since 1932.24
  • Iowa (11:47 PM): Josh Turek (D) 50.5, Ashley Hinson (R) 46.4. Open seat; Joni Ernst retired. CBS tied the result to affordability and tariffs.
  • Maine (12:52 AM): Troy Jackson (D) 53.6, Susan Collins (R) 46.3. Collins’s 30-year run ends.

Democratic holds that had been rated competitive: Georgia (Ossoff 59.0 over Mike Collins 41.0), New Hampshire (Pappas 56.2 over John Sununu 42.0, open seat), Michigan (El-Sayed 52.5 over Mike Rogers 45.5, open seat), Minnesota (Flanagan 53.7 over Tafoya 43.6, open seat). None was within 7 points.

Republican holds that got closer than expected: Louisiana (Julia Letlow 50.9 over Jamie Davis 49.1, after Letlow ousted Sen. Bill Cassidy in the GOP primary), the Florida special (Ashley Moody 50.9 over Angie Nixon 48.0), and Nebraska (Pete Ricketts 50.4 over independent Dan Osborn 43.7, not called until 4:02 AM).

Uncalled: Alaska (Peltola 48.2, Sullivan 47.9, with ranked-choice tabulation still to come) and Mississippi (Colom 48.95, Hyde-Smith 48.94, separated by 43 votes). Both are covered below.

The House: a five-seat GOP majority becomes a Democratic landslide

Republicans went into the night with 219 seats (counting Kevin Kiley, who left the party but caucused with them), Democrats with 214, and two vacancies.5 Democrats needed a net gain of three. By late Wednesday morning NBC’s tracker had them at 236 called seats to 146, with 53 uncalled and 31 flips so far, and its model projected a final total of at least 258. CBS’s estimate was 259, in a range of 251 to 268.105

Who lost. Wikipedia’s running tally counts 26 Republican incumbents defeated plus Kiley, and 10 Republican open seats taken by Democrats. No Democratic incumbent lost.11 CBS’s list of defeated Republicans includes:5

  • Pennsylvania, four seats: Brian Fitzpatrick (PA-1, to Bob Harvie), Ryan Mackenzie (PA-7, Bob Brooks), Rob Bresnahan (PA-8, Paige Cognetti) and Scott Perry (PA-10, Janelle Stelson, in a rematch). Fitzpatrick had won by nearly 13 points in 2024.
  • Iowa, all three GOP seats: Mariannette Miller-Meeks (IA-1, Christina Bohannan, in their third rematch), the open IA-2 (Lindsay James) and Zach Nunn (IA-3, Sarah Trone Garriott).
  • Wisconsin, two: Bryan Steil (WI-1, Mitchell Berman) and Derrick Van Orden (WI-3, Rebecca Cooke).
  • Ohio, two: Max Miller (OH-7, Brian Poindexter), who stayed in the race after domestic-abuse allegations he denied, and Mike Carey (OH-15, Don Leonard).
  • Elsewhere: Tom Kean Jr. (NJ-7, Rebecca Bennett), Gabe Evans (CO-8, Manny Rutinel), Jen Kiggans (VA-2, Elaine Luria), Ann Wagner (MO-2, Fred Wellman), Victoria Spartz (IN-5, J.D. Ford), Monica De La Cruz (TX-15, Bobby Pulido), James Gallagher (CA-1, Mike McGuire), Kiley (CA-6, Richard Pan) and David Valadao (CA-22, Randy Villegas, projected by NBC).10 Mike Lawler (NY-17) conceded to Cait Conley at 2:45 AM, though the race was still formally uncalled because of ballot-scanner problems in Westchester.
  • Open seats that flipped: NC-11 (Jamie Ager, the first Democrat since 2010), KY-6 (Zach Dembo), NE-2 (Denise Powell), TX-23 (Katy Padilla Stout), TX-35 (Johnny Garcia), UT-1 (Ben McAdams, in a court-ordered district) and MI-10 (Christina Hines).

The Democratic Women’s Caucus said more than 100 Democratic women were elected, up from 97, a first for the group. Colorado’s Melat Kiros, 29, becomes the first Gen Z woman in Congress after beating Rep. Diana DeGette in the July primary.5

Decided before November

Most of the map was never in play. Of the 35 Senate races, Cook rated 22 as Solid for one party going in, and 23 ended up decided by 10 points or more.89 In those states, the real election was the primary.

Senate seats where the primary was the contest:

  • Texas: Ken Paxton beat Sen. John Cornyn in the May 26 Republican runoff by more than 25 points after Trump endorsed him on May 19. Cornyn had led the March 3 first round.25 Paxton then lost the general by 12. Here the primary chose the nominee but did not settle the seat.
  • Louisiana: Rep. Julia Letlow, with Trump’s endorsement, knocked out Sen. Bill Cassidy in the state’s first closed party primary since 2010 (May 16: Letlow 44.8, John Fleming 28.3, Cassidy 24.8), then won the June 27 runoff with 56.9 percent. Forecasters called the seat safe, and Letlow held it by 1.8 points.26
  • Kentucky: Rep. Andy Barr took 60.5 percent in the May 19 GOP primary over former Attorney General Daniel Cameron for Mitch McConnell’s open seat, then won 55.0 to 42.9 over Charles Booker.27
  • Michigan: Abdul El-Sayed won the August 4 Democratic primary with 48.5 percent over Rep. Haley Stevens after Mallory McMorrow withdrew. Mike Rogers was the only Republican to qualify.28
  • Safe open seats settled in spring: Alabama (Barry Moore, 54.7), Montana (Kurt Alme, 49.0 over independent Seth Bodnar 37.3), Oklahoma (Kevin Hern, 57.3), Wyoming (Harriet Hageman, 65.2), Illinois (Juliana Stratton, 61.0) and Minnesota (Peggy Flanagan, 53.7).9
  • Kansas: Adam Hamilton won an eight-way Democratic primary with just 34.6 percent, then beat Sen. Roger Marshall.24

House seats with no real general election. 270toWin counts 18 districts with only one major party on the November ballot, down from 37 in 2024:12

  • Entirely unopposed (5): FL-10 Maxwell Frost, MA-2 Jim McGovern, MA-5 Katherine Clark, MA-7 Ayanna Pressley, WI-2 Mark Pocan.
  • No major-party opponent (4): LA-2 Troy Carter, MA-1 Richard Neal, NJ-8 Rob Menendez, PA-3 (open, won by Chris Rabb).
  • Same-party California top-two races (9): CA-4, CA-7, CA-11 (Pelosi’s open seat), CA-12, CA-14, CA-29, CA-34, CA-37, and CA-40, where Republicans Young Kim and Ken Calvert faced each other.

Sixty House members retired or ran for something else (26 Democrats, 36 Republicans), the second-most in any cycle since 1992.11

The close ones

Twelve Senate races finished inside 10 points, and two are still being counted.

Mississippi: 43 votes. With about 96 percent counted, Scott Colom (D) led Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith 462,821 to 462,778.1415 Colom’s campaign says 10,000 to 15,000 affidavit ballots remain, and Hyde-Smith’s says its numbers show her winning. Mississippi has no automatic or candidate-requested recount, so any challenge goes to court. Colom says he is prepared to go there.2 Cook had rated the seat Likely Republican.

Alaska: 0.3 points and a name on the ballot. Mary Peltola (D) led Sen. Dan Sullivan 102,701 to 102,075 in first choices, with Republican Gerald Heikes at 4,821 and an undeclared retired teacher named Dan J. Sullivan at 3,583.16 Tens of thousands of ballots were uncounted. Ranked-choice tabulation is set for November 18 and certification for November 25. The Trump administration sued to block certification over voter confusion with the namesake candidate, and a federal judge dismissed the suit without a hearing. The DOJ, FBI and state police opened investigations into reports that Democratic operatives recruited him; Peltola and the DSCC deny it. Spending topped $100 million, against $28 million in 2020. Ballot Measure 2, which would repeal ranked-choice voting, was itself too close to call at 48 to 52.17

The money. AdImpact projected a record $11.6 billion in political ads this cycle.18 By OpenSecrets’ method, counting candidate plus outside spending as of October 23, Texas became the most expensive Senate race ever at about $560 million, passing the $516 million Georgia 2020-21 runoff. Ohio was at $423 million, Michigan $337 million, Iowa $185 million, Georgia $179 million, Maine $172 million, North Carolina $131 million and Alaska $130 million.19 Talarico’s victory line, “Texas is not for sale,” was aimed at Elon Musk’s reported $150 million-plus for Paxton.7 In the House, AdImpact put NC-1 (Don Davis vs. Laurie Buckhout) over $52 million, the most expensive House race in history, followed by FL-25, MI-7, NY-17, MI-10, TX-34, WI-3, CO-8, FL-14 and CA-22.20

NBC listed 53 House seats uncalled at midday, including 9 in California, 5 each in Arizona and Florida, and 4 each in Texas and New York.10 Western states that vote by mail will take days, and CNN warned official results could take weeks.3

Heated for reasons beyond the margin: Texas (Musk money, Paxton’s scandals, a 30-year streak ending), Alaska (the namesake candidate and federal investigations), South Carolina (a sister appointed to her late brother’s seat), Ohio’s Max Miller (abuse allegations), New Jersey’s Tom Kean Jr. (months of missed votes during undisclosed depression treatment), and Nebraska, where independent Dan Osborn held Pete Ricketts under 51 percent.

Governors and ballot measures

Governors. Democrats flipped Texas (Gina Hinojosa 52.8 over Gov. Greg Abbott 45.7, ending 30 years of GOP control), Georgia (Keisha Lance Bottoms 54.3 over Rick Jackson), Nevada (Aaron Ford 51.7 over Gov. Joe Lombardo), Ohio (Amy Acton 52.3 over Vivek Ramaswamy 44.4) and Iowa (Rob Sand 55.5 over Zach Lahn). They now hold a majority of governorships for the first time in 16 years.222 Alaska (Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins, D, leading) and Kansas (Cindy Holscher, D, 50.26 to Ty Masterson 49.74) were uncalled.

The closest governor races were Kansas (0.5), Florida (1.3, Byron Donalds over David Jolly), Tennessee (2.5, Marsha Blackburn over Jerri Green), New Hampshire (4.0, Kelly Ayotte), Alabama (4.1, Tommy Tuberville over Doug Jones) and South Carolina (4.5, Alan Wilson).22

Two patterns stand out. Voter ID and citizenship requirements passed everywhere they were called, often in the same states that elected Democrats. And Missouri voters both rejected the legislature’s mid-decade congressional map and refused to roll back the abortion rights they approved in 2024.
