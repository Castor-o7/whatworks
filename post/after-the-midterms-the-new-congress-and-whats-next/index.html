After the midterms: the new Congress, and what happens between now and January

Two days on, Democrats hold 54 Senate seats and are headed for about 259 in the House. Here is the full map of both chambers, the lame-duck deadlines before the handoff, and the race to lead each party, starting with Speaker-in-waiting Hakeem Jeffries.

By Josh · November 5, 2026

Two days after the election, the outcome is settled and the margins are still moving. Democrats have clinched the Senate with 54 seats and lead the House with 242 called seats against 151. Forty-two House races and two Senate races are still being counted.12 This follow-up to Wednesday’s results post covers three things: what both chambers will look like in January, what the outgoing Republican Congress still has to do before then, and who will run each party in the 120th Congress.

Chamber Today (119th Congress) Called as of Nov. 5, 10 AM ET Projected for January (120th)
Senate (100) 53 R, 47 D 54 D, 44 R, 2 uncalled 54 to 56 D, depending on Alaska and Mississippi
House (435) 219 R, 214 D, 2 vacant 242 D, 151 R, 42 uncalled NBC estimate: 259 D, 176 R

Every number here is unofficial. States certify over the next few weeks, and each chamber is the final judge of its own members’ elections.3

The full map: the Senate

Wednesday’s map showed only the 35 seats on the ballot. This one shows all 100: each state’s two senators as they will stand when the new Congress meets.

The flips turned eight states into split delegations: Iowa, Kansas, North Carolina, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas now each have one senator from each party, joining Pennsylvania and Wisconsin. Maine goes from split to two members of the Democratic caucus: Troy Jackson plus independent Angus King. Two seats, both now held by Republicans, are still in doubt.1

Mississippi: the lead grew, and the count isn’t over. On Wednesday morning Scott Colom (D) led Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by 43 votes. By the end of counting Wednesday it was 1,138: 464,926 (49.0 percent) to 463,788 (48.9), with independent Ty Pinkins at 19,934 and about 96 percent of the expected vote in.45 Twelve counties, mostly around Jackson and Gulfport, are still reporting. The secretary of state counts about 3,600 mail ballots that can still arrive. They count if postmarked by Election Day and received by 5 PM on Nov. 10. Voters with affidavit (provisional) ballots have until Nov. 10 to fix problems, and nobody knows how many there are. Colom’s campaign estimates 15,000, and the state says it has no definitive number. Counties must certify by Nov. 13.64

Mississippi has no recount. Candidates can’t request one, and no margin triggers one automatically. A candidate can examine ballot boxes county by county after certification and go to court if they find problems. An Ole Miss election-law attorney told NBC that across 82 counties “that’s beyond the scope of feasibility.”46 Hyde-Smith’s campaign says a full count will reelect her. Colom says he is prepared to litigate.

Alaska: ranked choice decides it. With about three-quarters of the vote in, Mary Peltola (D) led Sen. Dan Sullivan by just over 600 first-choice votes, 48.2 to 47.9 percent.71 Mail ballots from remote areas can arrive up to 15 days after Election Day. If no one reaches a majority, the last-place candidates are eliminated and their ballots move to voters’ next choices. That tabulation is scheduled for Nov. 18, with certification on Nov. 25.8 On Thursday morning President Trump posted that votes for the “Fake Dan Sullivan,” the unrelated candidate with the same name, “should be immediately given to the REAL Dan Sullivan.” No court or election official has acted on that.7

Kansas’s governor race is also still open. Cindy Holscher (D) led Ty Masterson by more than 7,000 votes with 99 percent in.7

The full map: the House

By NBC’s tally, Democrats have flipped 35 Republican-held seats and lost one, a net gain of 34 so far. NBC’s model projects 259 Democratic seats, an 83-seat majority, and at least 258.2 For comparison, Republicans won 220 seats in 2024. Some of the trade press is already calling it a “blue tsunami.”9

Since Wednesday’s post, the AP called Michigan’s 10th for Democrat Christina Hines, a flip, over Mike Bouchard.10 Most of the 42 uncalled seats are in states that count mail ballots for days: California, Arizona, Florida, New York, Colorado and Washington. Those states will decide whether the final number lands closer to 250 or 265.

The lame duck: what the outgoing Congress still has to do

Republicans still control both chambers until noon on Jan. 3. Congress returns for its lame-duck session on Nov. 9 and is expected to work until Christmas, with a Thanksgiving break.11 Most of what it has to do runs into two dates:

The big question is how much gets done now and how much waits for January. Each side has reasons to wait or to move:

  • Republicans’ incentives: This is their last month with the gavels. Expect a push to confirm President Trump’s nominees, including several at Health and Human Services, before Democrats take over the Senate and its confirmation calendar.21 Some Republicans have floated a third budget-reconciliation package that would raise the debt limit for the rest of Trump’s term and add defense money. No package is settled, and some GOP aides expect it to slip to January.1211
  • Democrats’ incentives: In January they will hold both chambers, so they have less reason to strike broad deals now.12 Before the election, Jeffries said he favored finishing the full-year spending bills in the lame duck rather than punting them.22 Whether he still feels that way with an 80-plus-seat majority waiting is one of the first things to watch next week.

The pre-election analyses disagree on the outcome. Congress could pass another short-term funding bill that hands the fight to the new Congress, or it could finish full-year bills now, with a health extenders package riding on either.1112

The new Congress: who runs what

The calendar.

  • Nov. 9: Lame-duck session begins.11
  • Nov. 10: Republican leadership elections in both chambers. Senate Republicans meet at 9 AM in the Old Senate Chamber. House Republicans hold a two-hour candidate forum that morning and vote that afternoon for leader, whip and conference chair.23 Mississippi’s mail-ballot and affidavit deadlines fall the same day.
  • Nov. 13: Mississippi counties certify.6
  • Nov. 18 and 25: Alaska’s ranked-choice tabulation, then certification.8
  • Dec. 11: Government funding runs out.
  • Jan. 3, 2027, noon: The 120th Congress convenes, as the 20th Amendment requires unless Congress sets another day by law. Jan. 3 is a Sunday this time, so a law moving the date is possible.3 The House’s first order of business is electing a Speaker.

Speaker of the House: Hakeem Jeffries. The Brooklyn Democrat has led House Democrats since 2023 and is expected to be the next Speaker. He would be the first Black Speaker in U.S. history.13 The formal steps: House Democrats nominate him in a closed caucus vote (we haven’t seen a date announced as of Thursday), and then on Jan. 3 the full House votes by roll call, every member saying a name aloud. The winner needs a majority of those voting for a person. With roughly 259 Democrats, Jeffries could lose dozens of votes and still win on the first ballot. That’s a big change from the two most recent Speaker fights: Kevin McCarthy’s 15-ballot election in 2023 and Mike Johnson’s narrow first-ballot win in 2025. No Democrat has said publicly they will challenge him. Katherine Clark, now the Democratic whip, is expected to stay in senior leadership. Who fills the No. 2 and No. 3 jobs under a Speaker Jeffries hasn’t been reported yet.13

House Republicans: Mike Johnson steps back. Johnson told staff on election night that he won’t run for minority leader.14 Candidates had until noon Nov. 6 to declare.15 Rep. Jim Jordan of Ohio and Majority Leader Steve Scalise of Louisiana are the names most often reported, but as of Thursday morning neither had formally announced. President Trump said of the race, “We’ll stay out of it.”15 The vote is Nov. 10.

The Senate: Schumer back to majority leader. Chuck Schumer is expected to become majority leader again, swapping places with John Thune.13 Democrats also need a new No. 2. Whip Dick Durbin is retiring, and Juliana Stratton won his Illinois seat on Tuesday.16 Brian Schatz of Hawaii, now chief deputy whip, has Schumer’s endorsement for whip and was reported in 2025 to have locked up the votes.1718

On the Republican side, Thune’s office says he will run for minority leader, and no one has publicly challenged him. Rick Scott said in September he would not run against him again. Whip John Barrasso is expected to stay No. 2, James Lankford to seek another term as conference vice chair, and Shelley Moore Capito to stay Policy Committee chair.19

What divided government looks like from here. For the last two years of Trump’s term, Democrats will control what comes to the floor, the committee gavels and subpoenas, and the Senate’s confirmation calendar. The president keeps the veto and executive action. K&L Gates summed it up the day after the election: Democrats “can thwart the Administration’s priorities, conduct investigations, and advance their own legislative agenda,” while Trump “will continue to act through Executive Action” and “will be able to use his veto power.”20 No Democratic bill becomes law without either Trump’s signature or two-thirds of both chambers, and Democrats have nowhere near two-thirds of either. In practice, the next two years will mostly be about oversight, must-pass spending deals, and messaging bills that set up 2028.

The first test comes before any of that. The Dec. 11 funding deadline falls inside the lame duck, so the outgoing Republican majority has to keep the government open while the incoming Democratic one decides how much leverage to save for January.
